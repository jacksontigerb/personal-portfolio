A PFAS free water repellent coating in collaboration with Finisterre

My Master's project, supervised by Prospero Taroni-Junior and run with Abbie Spence at Finisterre. I made a PFAS free water repellent coating for outdoor fabric and tested it against the finish they already use.

Read the full case studyInteractive results

a droplet going onto a coated sample in the goniometer

the rig itself · a droplet going down onto a coated sample

Every sample is measured the same way. A droplet goes onto the fabric from the needle, the backlight shows its outline, and the contact angle is read from that outline. A higher angle means the surface repels water more.

PFAS are the fluorine chemicals used in most water repellent outdoor gear, and they're linked to harm to people and the environment. The hard part is making a fluorine free finish that still beads water after it's been washed and worn.

Finisterre already make their kit with PFAS free chemistry, so their own finish on their own fabric was the benchmark. My coating went onto cotton, and onto the same Finisterre fabric supplied without a finish.

SEM at ×2,000 · modifying the particle surface broke up the clumps, but it did not make the coating repel or shed water any better

static contact angle before wear · 132 to 135° for my coatings on cotton and 132° for the commercial finish on Finisterre’s fabric, while uncoated cotton wets out at 38.8°

I measured roll off as well as contact angle, then tested the samples through 200 sandpaper abrasion cycles and 20 lab wash cycles. Only C-C3 on cotton ended washing near its starting roll off angle. On the same Finisterre fabric, my emulsion and the commercial finish both ended near 80°, so I can't claim mine lasts better through washing. High contact angles also didn't stop small drops from pinning.

Battery ageing model Huawei Tech Arena 2026, Digital Power

A grid storage battery loses capacity slowly for years, then the loss suddenly speeds up and the pack reaches end of life soon after. The competition asked for the whole curve, down through that knee to 70% state of health, at temperatures and charge rates the model hadn't seen, using only the temperature and the C rate. There were no sensor traces, because the cells being scored didn't have any.

The organisers' baseline fades as n to the power 0.8. That exponent is below one, so the fade rate falls as the cell ages, and a knee is the opposite: fade that accelerates. The baseline couldn't represent what the task was asking for, so I modelled the fade and the knee separately and let the knee fit to zero where a cell didn't have one.

Two things mattered most. The knee doesn't follow the Arrhenius law the fade does, and the dominant ageing mechanism in these cells changes somewhere around 35 to 45°C, so one temperature law couldn't cover both. None of the open datasets carried the condition dependence the six unseen operating points needed, so it had to come from the target data, with the open data used only as a prior on curve shape.

The organisers keep their scoring metric hidden, so designing the validation was part of the task. Six cells were released, one per operating condition. Each fold held one whole condition out and trained on the other five, since part of the hidden scoring is at conditions with no released data. Against the baseline at 1.000, lower being better, the mean over the six folds was 0.711, and the error at the 70% end of life point fell from 64% to 36%.

Six folds make that number loose. Dropping any one condition moves the mean anywhere from 0.44 to 0.99, and most of the remaining error sits at the hottest condition, 55°C at 1C, where the model has to extrapolate in temperature. These are held out results on the released cells, not the organisers’ leaderboard score.

How fast a silicate takes up CO₂
solid state synthesis / XRD / Curtin University, Perth
How fast a silicate takes up CO₂ visiting research associate, Curtin University

I’m at Curtin in Perth until November 2026, on the reaction that lets silicate minerals lock carbon away. Silicates weather naturally and take up CO₂ as they go, but slowly, and for the one I’m working on nobody has measured how slowly. That is the project: the rate, and whether the process would scale.

First I make strontium orthosilicate (Sr₂SiO₄) by solid state reaction. Strontium carbonate and silica are weighed out, ball milled together under acetone, dried, then fired in a furnace. XRD says whether the phase I wanted is the phase I got.

Then I carbonate it. The powder goes into water with CO₂ bubbled through, and samples come out at set times to be filtered, rinsed and dried. Full carbonation adds 0.329 g of CO₂ per gram of Sr₂SiO₄, so the mass gain measures how far the reaction has gone, with XRD confirming the strontium carbonate and a water only run alongside as the control. Designing that experiment is part of the work, since there is no method to copy.

It is a model system rather than carbon removal in itself. Making Sr₂SiO₄ from strontium carbonate releases the same CO₂ the carbonation later takes back, so the value is in the rate and in what it says about doing this at the scale of a mine site.

There's nothing to report yet.

Redox flow batteries
energy storage / expert interviews / University of Exeter
Redox flow batteries BEng dissertation, awarded a first

My BEng dissertation at Exeter, supervised by Xiaohong Li and David Trudgeon. Wind and solar generate when the weather allows, so the grid needs storage that can hold several hours and give it back slowly. Flow batteries keep the energy in external electrolyte tanks and the power in a separate stack, so the two can be sized independently.

I compared five chemistries: zinc bromine, iron chromium, metal air, lithium based and vanadium. Vanadium came out ahead for grid scale. All four of its oxidation states dissolve in the same acid, so electrolyte crossing between the half cells only self discharges instead of contaminating them, and a stack takes 10,000 to 20,000 deep cycles before the capacity noticeably fades. The electrolyte is water based, so there is no thermal runaway to design around.

I also ran my own interviews. Seven invitations went out to people working on vanadium flow batteries in industry and research, four agreed, and three recordings survived a fault that lost the fourth. Twelve questions each, twenty to forty minutes, transcribed and then corrected by hand.

They agreed on the barriers. Perfluorinated membranes such as Nafion are expensive, pumps and power electronics pull real round trip efficiency down to about 75%, and market rules reward fast response rather than the eight hour discharge vanadium is suited to. The Energy Superhub Oxford case study showed the pairing that gets used in practice: the vanadium system absorbs the daily deep cycling so the lithium pack next to it can take the short high power calls and last longer.

None of those are scientific problems. Vanadium already meets the technical requirements for pairing with wind and solar, and what's left is cost and regulation.
